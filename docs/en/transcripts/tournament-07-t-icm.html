ICM: Why Chips Are Not Prize Money

Use a full numeric example with three players to see risk premium.

Takeaway: ICM is an approximate model of prize equity; equal chips do not guarantee equal prize returns.

  • Live tournament
  • Chips and ante as per problem statement
  • Strategy must align with reward structure

1. Start with a simplified model

Three players, each with 10 chips, prize distributed as 50, 30, 20.

Three players already have rewards. Ignore blinds, antes, and other investments, and estimate prize equity as ICM. Exclude technical advantages and future blind changes. With equal stacks, current equity per player is 100/3≈33.3.

Full spoken explanation

Three players are already in the money. We temporarily ignore blinds, antes, skill differences and future changes, and use the Independent Chip Model to estimate prize equity. All three have equal stacks, and the prizes total one hundred, so each player's current estimated equity is about thirty three point three.

2. Losing the hand, you get third-place prize

You face all-in against A with 10 chips, B is not involved; after loss, prize is 20.

Compare the two outcomes starting from the current point. When you lose all your chips, you receive a guaranteed third-place prize of 20, which is below your current estimated equity of 33.3.

3. Win the pot but equity doesn't double

You win the A after having 20, B has 10; guaranteed second place.

In the two-player stage, the ICM model gives you a 20/30 probability of finishing first. Prize equity is (2/3)×50+(1/3)×30≈43.3. That is only 10 more than the original 33.3, compared with a loss of about 13.3 if you lose.

You win the A after having 20, B has 10; guaranteed second place.

Full spoken explanation

With two players remaining, you have twenty chips and your opponent has ten. The model gives you a two thirds chance of finishing first and a one third chance of finishing second. Weighting the prizes of fifty and thirty gives equity of about forty three point three. That is only ten above the original value, less than the thirteen point three you lose when you lose the confrontation.

4. Separate chip and prize thresholds

No other pot in this example; chip threshold 50%; prize threshold approximately 57.1%

If folding and both have 10, let winning probability be p. Prize break-even condition is p×43.3+(1−p)×20=33.3; exact solution is p=4/7≈57.1%. Ignoring ties, values come from this specific model.

If folding leaves three players with ten chips each, weight the prize equities after winning and losing to find the break even calling probability. The exact threshold is four sevenths, about fifty seven point one percent. This threshold belongs to this simplified example; it is not a universal tournament rule.

6. The model is not a universal answer

True calculation still requires remaining chip distribution, reward structure, and specific actions.

ICM does not directly include every skill edge, future position or change in the blinds. It helps explain the cost of elimination; it does not mean folding every hand on the bubble. Risk premiums differ across players and matchups.
